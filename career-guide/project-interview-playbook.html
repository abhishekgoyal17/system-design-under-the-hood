Part I — The method

01Why projects decide offers

DSA gets you through the door. The project round decides your level.

In almost every loop — SDE-2, Senior, Staff, Hiring Manager, Bar Raiser — someone says the same sentence: "Walk me through a project you're proud of." It sounds like the easiest question in the loop. It's actually the one where the interviewer learns the most about you, because it's the only question where you pick the topic and they get to watch how deep you can go.

Most candidates lose this round for one of three reasons. None of them is "lack of skill".

01

They only know their slice

You fixed a race condition in one consumer. The interviewer asks "how does the message get to that consumer in the first place?" — and you go quiet. Knowing only your diff makes a real contribution sound small.

02

They have no structure

The story comes out in the order they remember it, not the order a listener needs it. Two minutes in, the interviewer still doesn't know what the system does, why it mattered, or which part was yours.

03

They get ambushed by counter-questions

"Why not Kafka exactly-once?" "What happens if Redis is down?" "How did you measure that 40%?" Each of these is predictable from what you said — if you'd prepared for it.

If you can't explain the system around your change, the interviewer assumes you couldn't have designed the change either.

02You own the story

The interviewer asks the questions. You decide what they're about.

A project explanation isn't a test you sit — it's something you design. You choose which details go in, in what order, and which ones you leave half-open so the interviewer follows them into an area where you're strong. The same project can sound like a junior task or a senior design depending only on how you frame it.

Sounds like a ticket
"I worked on the notification service. There was a bug where some emails were going twice, so I added a check using Redis and it got fixed. I also wrote some tests."

No system, no cause, no why, no number, no hook. The interviewer has to pull every detail out of you, and nothing invites a follow-up you're ready for.

Sounds like ownership
"Our order pipeline is at-least-once — Kafka can redeliver during consumer rebalances. That was sending ~2% duplicate confirmation emails. I traced it to non-idempotent consumers and added a Redis SETNX idempotency key with a 24h TTL, so duplicates dropped to near zero without adding latency."

Same work. But now the system, root cause, mechanism and impact are all there, and every highlighted phrase is a hook that leads to a question you've already prepared.

The running example in this guide

To keep things concrete, this playbook uses one made-up project throughout: "Fixed duplicate order-confirmation emails in a Kafka-based Notification Service using Redis idempotency keys." It's small on purpose — a bug fix, not a platform rewrite — because that's exactly the kind of project most people under-sell. Swap in your own project everywhere you see it.

03From bug fix to end-to-end

You don't need to have built the whole system. You need to understand it.

Most real work is a slice of something bigger: a bug, an enhancement, one module in a large product. That's fine — and it's honest to say so. What isn't fine is not knowing what's around your slice. Prepare in four concentric rings and label each one clearly so you never over-claim:

RingDepth you needTypical question that tests it
Owned ChangeLine-by-line, every alternative considered"Why SETNX and not a DB unique constraint?"
Contributed ComponentEvery class/API/table on your path"What happens to the message after your check passes?"
Context SystemWhiteboard-level: boxes, arrows, protocols"Where does the event come from? Who else consumes it?"
Context BusinessUsers, cost, metrics, before/after"How did you know this was worth fixing?"
Honesty is a feature, not a weakness

Saying "The consumer framework already existed — my part was making it idempotent, but here's how the whole pipeline works" scores higher than claiming you built everything. Clear ownership + broad understanding is exactly what senior engineers sound like. Over-claiming collapses on the third follow-up.

04Steering the interview with hooks

If you say this, they will ask that. Plan both.

Interviewers follow the most interesting noun in your last sentence. That makes the conversation predictable. A hook is a specific, slightly-open term you deliberately place in your pitch because you've prepared a strong answer for the question it triggers. Good hooks pull the interview toward your strengths; accidental hooks pull it toward your gaps.

Try it: click each highlighted hook in the pitch below to see the question it triggers and the prepared answer behind it.

"Our order pipeline is , so Kafka can redeliver during . That was causing . I made the consumer idempotent with a and a , and we chose to if Redis is unavailable."

← Click a hook to see the question it plants.

Rules for planting hooks

  • Plant 4–6 hooks in your 2-minute pitch. Fewer and the interviewer picks topics at random; more and it sounds like buzzword soup.
  • Every hook needs a prepared answer that's 60–90 seconds long and itself contains one deeper hook. This is how you chain two or three levels deep on your terms.
  • Remove accidental hooks. If you say "we used Cassandra" but can't explain partition keys, either learn it or don't say it.
  • Hook your tradeoffs. Phrases like "we chose X over Y" or "we deliberately didn't…" are the strongest hooks, because they invite a design discussion — the senior-level signal.

Part II — The blueprint

06Collect raw material first

AI can structure and explain. It cannot remember what you did.

The quality of the handbook is capped by the quality of what you feed in. Spend 60–90 minutes gathering this before you open any AI tool. Paste it as rough notes — it doesn't need to be tidy.

  • 1
    The ask and the whyTicket / epic / PRD text, customer complaint, incident report, or the Slack thread that started it. What hurt, and for whom?
  • 2
    Your actual diffsPR titles + descriptions, the class/method names you touched, key snippets (sanitized), review comments that changed your design.
  • 3
    The surrounding systemArchitecture docs, READMEs, runbooks, config files, API contracts, table schemas — anything that explains the components around yours.
  • 4
    NumbersBefore/after metrics, dashboards, load numbers, latency, error rate, cost, customer count, time saved. Even rough ranges ("~2%", "40+ customers") are fine — mark which are exact.
  • 5
    Decisions and rejected optionsWhat else did you or the team consider? What did a reviewer push back on? Rejected options are where the best interview answers come from.
  • 6
    What went wrongBugs you hit, rollbacks, wrong first hypotheses, production surprises. These become your war stories.
  • 7
    Tests, rollout, monitoringHow you tested, feature flags, canary/rollout plan, alerts and dashboards you added.
  • 8
    Target interviewCompany, level, round type (project deep dive, LLD, HLD, HM, behavioral). The handbook emphasis changes with this.
Confidentiality — non-negotiable

Never paste proprietary source code, customer names, credentials, internal URLs or unreleased product details into a public AI tool. Use your company's approved AI tooling, or sanitize first: rename internal services to generic roles ("Ingestion Service"), round numbers, and describe code in pseudocode. The sanitizer prompt in Part III helps. In the interview itself, the same rule applies — describe patterns, not secrets.

08Pitch engineering

One story, three lengths, same skeleton.

Every version of the pitch follows the same seven beats. The 30-second version gives each beat one clause; the 5-minute version gives each one a paragraph and a diagram. Because the skeleton is the same, you can stretch or shrink depending on how much time the interviewer gives you.

Context→ Problem→ My role→ Approach→ Hard part→ Impact→ Hook
Use when: "tell me about yourself", recruiter screen, or the first answer in a HM round.

"I worked on the Notification Service in our e-commerce platform. Customers were getting duplicate order-confirmation emails — about 2% of sends — because our Kafka pipeline is at-least-once. I traced it to consumer rebalances and made the consumer idempotent using Redis keys with a TTL. Duplicates dropped to near zero with no added latency, and the pattern was reused by two other teams."

Use when: "walk me through a project". This is the version you rehearse most.

[Context] "Our checkout publishes an OrderPlaced event to Kafka, and a Notification Service consumes it to send confirmation emails — around 1.2 million a day. [Problem] Support was seeing complaints about duplicate emails; when I measured, about 2% of orders got two or more. [Role] I owned the investigation and the fix inside the Notification Service; the Kafka setup and email provider integration already existed. [Approach] I correlated duplicates with consumer-group rebalances during deploys — offsets weren't committed before partitions moved, so another instance re-processed them. Rather than chase exactly-once, I made the consumer idempotent: before sending, it does a Redis SET-NX on a key derived from orderId and template, with a 24-hour TTL. [Hard part] The tricky bit was what happens if the send fails after we've claimed the key — so the key has a state and failed sends release it. [Impact] Duplicates went from ~2% to under 0.01%, p99 latency added was about 1ms, and two other teams adopted the same helper. [Hook] The interesting decision was choosing to fail open when Redis is down — happy to go into why."

Use when: a dedicated project deep-dive round. Draw while you talk.
  1. Business context (30s) — what the platform does, scale, who uses notifications.
  2. Architecture (60s) — draw §05: Checkout → Order Service → Kafka → Notification consumers → Template service → Email provider.
  3. Problem & diagnosis (60s) — symptoms, how you measured 2%, the wrong first hypothesis (provider retries), the real cause (rebalance before offset commit).
  4. Options considered (45s) — Kafka exactly-once, DB unique constraint, Redis idempotency key, provider-side dedup. Why Redis won.
  5. Design (60s) — draw §06 sequence diagram: key format, SETNX + TTL, claim/sent state, release on failure.
  6. Edge cases (30s) — Redis down → fail open; TTL vs late redelivery; hot keys.
  7. Rollout & impact (30s) — flag, shadow mode counting would-be duplicates, then enforce; metrics.
  8. Reflection (15s) — what you'd do differently; open the floor with a hook.
Copy this into your notes and fill the brackets for every project.
"I worked on [system], which [does what, at what scale].
The problem was [symptom + who it hurt + number].
My part was [exact scope]; [what already existed / what the team did].
I [diagnosed / designed / built] [approach], choosing [X] over [Y] because [reason].
The hard part was [edge case / constraint], which I handled by [mechanism].
As a result [metric before → after], and [secondary impact].
The most interesting part was [hook] — happy to go deeper."
Say it out loud, not in your head

Time yourself. Most people's "2-minute" pitch is 4 minutes long and front-loads context. If the interviewer doesn't know what you did by the 45-second mark, cut context.

09The scope honesty matrix

Pre-decide your "I" and "we" so you never have to improvise them.

Interviewers are trained to probe "what did you do?". Ambiguity reads as over-claiming. Build this table for every project and rehearse the phrasing in the last column.

Piece of workLabelHow you say it
Root-cause analysis of duplicatesOwned"I traced it to rebalances by correlating duplicate timestamps with deploy events."
Idempotency helper + state handlingOwned"I designed and built the helper, including the failure-release path."
Fail-open decisionContributed"I proposed it; we agreed on it with the team lead and product after looking at the tradeoff."
Kafka cluster, consumer frameworkContext"That already existed — but here's how it works and why it was at-least-once."
Email provider integrationContext"Another team owns it; I worked against its API contract."
Blurry ownership
"We built the notification pipeline and we fixed duplicates using Redis…"

Interviewer can't tell if you led it or watched it. Expect "what exactly did you do?" — now you're on the back foot.

Crisp ownership
"The pipeline existed. I owned the duplicate investigation and the idempotency fix end to end; the fail-open policy was a team decision I proposed."

Three ownership levels in one sentence. Honest, specific, and it still sounds like leadership.

Example: animated sequence diagram (happy path + redelivery)

This is the standard to aim for: numbered steps, request vs response arrows, the failure moment highlighted, and a replay button so you can watch the flow while rehearsing your narration.

Example: state machine for a non-obvious edge case

The sequence diagram shows the happy path. A state diagram is how you answer the inevitable "but what if the send fails after you've set the key?"

The 60-second whiteboard test

For each diagram in your handbook: close the file, set a 60-second timer, and redraw it on paper. Anything you couldn't reproduce is something you'll stumble on when explaining. Ask AI to simplify the diagram until you can draw it — the interviewer wants the shape, not every box.

The magic phrase

"We chose X over Y because Z, and we accepted W as the cost." Every tradeoff answer that includes the cost you accepted sounds two levels more senior than one that doesn't.

12The question ladder — 7 levels deep

Interviewers drill. Prepare at every depth, not just the top.

Questions about a project come in predictable levels. A good handbook has at least 4 questions per level — 30+ total — each with a spoken answer, why they're asking, the follow-up you'll get next, and the trap to avoid.

L1

Clarifying — "what is it?"

Checks that you can explain the system to an outsider.

"What does the Notification Service do? Who calls it?"
L2

Design — "how does it work?"

Architecture, data flow, components, APIs.

"Walk me through what happens when an order is placed."
L3

Deep dive — "why exactly like this?"

Algorithms, data structures, key formats, concurrency, code-level detail.

"Why SET NX EX instead of GET then SET? What's the key format?"
L4

Counter / trap — "why not X?"

Challenges your choices, often with a "better" alternative to see if you defend or fold.

"Why not just enable Kafka exactly-once semantics?"
L5

What-if / scale — "what breaks?"

10× load, component failure, multi-region, data growth.

"What if Redis goes down? What at 50M emails/day? Multi-region?"
L6

Behavioral — "how did you work?"

Conflict, ambiguity, mistakes, influence, ownership.

"Did anyone disagree with fail-open? How did you convince them?"
L7

Reflection — "what now?"

Retrospective, limitations, how you'd redo it, how it applies to their company.

"What would you do differently? How would this apply to our system?"

What a complete answer entry looks like

Each Q&A in your handbook should follow this format. Expand one to see it:

L4Why not just enable Kafka exactly-once semantics?

Spoken answer: "Kafka's exactly-once covers reading from and writing to Kafka within a transaction — it doesn't cover side-effects outside Kafka, like calling an email provider over HTTP. The send can succeed and the consumer can still crash before committing, so we'd still re-send. The only place to guarantee 'once' for an external side-effect is to make the side-effect itself idempotent, which is what the key does. EOS would also have added transactional overhead on every message for no real gain here."

Why they ask: To check if you understand delivery semantics, or just picked the first tool that worked.

Next follow-up: "So how would you get exactly-once end-to-end?" → outbox pattern / idempotent receivers / provider-side idempotency keys.

Trap to avoid: Saying "EOS is slow" without explaining why it doesn't solve the problem. Performance is a secondary argument; correctness is the primary one.

L5What happens if Redis is down?

Spoken answer: "We fail open: if the Redis call times out (we set 50ms), we send the email anyway and emit a metric. The reasoning is asymmetric cost — a missing order confirmation causes support tickets and anxiety, an occasional duplicate is an annoyance. We alert if the fail-open rate goes above a threshold. For an OTP or payment notification I'd flip that and fail closed with a retry queue."

Why they ask: Every new dependency is a new failure mode. They want to see you thought about it before they asked.

Next follow-up: "How is Redis deployed? What about failover losing keys?" → replica promotion can lose recent writes → small duplicate window, acceptable by the same cost logic.

Trap to avoid: "Redis never goes down." Never claim a dependency is perfectly reliable.

L6Tell me about a time something went wrong on this project.

Spoken answer (STAR-L): "My first hypothesis was wrong — I spent two days assuming the provider was retrying on timeouts. When I finally correlated duplicate timestamps with our deploy log, 90% clustered within 2 minutes of a rollout, which pointed to rebalances. The learning I took was: correlate with change events first before diving into a component. I now do that as step one in every incident."

Why they ask: Self-awareness and learning speed, not perfection.

Trap to avoid: A "mistake" that's actually a humble-brag, or blaming someone else.

Universal question bank — every project must answer these

Context & scope

  • What problem did this solve, and for whom?
  • How did you know it was a problem? What data?
  • What exactly was your contribution?
  • Who else was involved and what did they do?
  • How long did it take? Why that long?
  • What would have happened if no one fixed it?

Design & depth

  • Draw the architecture. Where is your part?
  • Walk me through one request end to end.
  • What alternatives did you consider?
  • What's the data model? Why those keys?
  • Which part was hardest technically?
  • Show me the core logic in pseudocode.

Failure & scale

  • What happens when dependency X fails?
  • What breaks at 10× traffic? 100×?
  • Any race conditions? How did you handle them?
  • How is it tested? What isn't tested?
  • How did you roll it out safely?
  • How would you know it's broken in production?

Impact & reflection

  • How did you measure impact? Before vs after?
  • What would you do differently now?
  • Did anyone disagree? How was it resolved?
  • What did you learn that you still use?
  • What's the next improvement you'd make?
  • How would you build this at our company?

You will get a question you didn't prepare. Prepare for that too.

The honest "I don't know" script

"I didn't work on that layer directly, so I don't want to guess at the specifics. My understanding is [principle-level answer]. If I needed to confirm, I'd check [where]." — This is a positive signal. Bluffing, then getting caught, is the single fastest way to fail a project round.

14STAR-L war stories

The technical round and the behavioral round use the same project.

Pull 2–3 stories from every project so you can answer behavioral questions with the same material. Add an L (Learning) to STAR — it's what turns a story into a signal of growth.

Part III — The prompts

15The Master Prompt

Copy it, fill the brackets, paste your raw material underneath.

This prompt turns any capable LLM (or coding agent with file access) into a principal engineer + interview coach + technical writer. It runs in two phases on purpose: first an outline and steering map you can correct, then the full HTML handbook. Skipping phase 1 is the most common reason AI output ends up generic.

Before you paste
  • Replace every [[…]] in the INPUTS block. Leave "unknown" rather than deleting a field.
  • Paste raw material (§06) at the end, after === RAW MATERIAL ===. Messy is fine; sanitized is mandatory.
  • Use the strongest model available, ideally one with a large context window. If it truncates, reply continue from <section id>.
  • If you're using a coding agent with repo access, add: "Explore the codebase to trace the flow yourself; cite file paths."

   Write my answers in first person with precise "I" vs "we".
R3 END-TO-END DEPTH. Even if I only fixed one bug, explain the whole system around it in four
   rings: my change -> my component -> the full system -> the business impact.
R4 SPOKEN, NOT WRITTEN. Pitches and answers must sound natural when said aloud: short sentences,
   no bullet-speak, 60-120 words per answer unless noted.
R5 STEERING. Deliberately plant 4-6 "hooks" (specific terms, tradeoffs, numbers) in the pitch.
   For each hook, prepare the question it triggers, the answer, and the next-level hook.
R6 TEACH THE FUNDAMENTALS. For every technology or concept I mention (e.g. Kafka offsets, Redis
   SETNX, B-tree indexes, consistent hashing), add a short "Fundamentals you must know" box
   with the 3-5 facts an interviewer will expect me to know.
R7 NO SECRETS. If confidentiality = yes, replace internal service/team/customer names with
   generic role names and keep a mapping table only at the very end, marked PRIVATE.

19The 7-day rehearsal plan

Per project. Roughly 60–90 minutes a day.

Day 1Collect & outline

Gather raw material (§06), sanitize it, run Master Prompt Phase 1. Answer the clarifying questions honestly.

Day 2Generate & verify

Run Phase 2. Read every line. Resolve every [[TODO]] and verify every "Inferred" claim with docs, code or a colleague.

Day 3Learn the rings

Study §03 fundamentals and §05 architecture. Redraw all diagrams from memory until each takes under 60 seconds.

Day 4Pitch ladder

Record yourself giving the 30s and 2-min pitch. Run the Pitch Tightener prompt on the transcript. Repeat until it flows.

Day 5Defend

Run the Counter-question Stress Test. Rehearse every L4 and L5 answer out loud. Fill MISSING answers into the handbook.

Day 6Mock interview

Two AI mock interviews with different "interviewer personalities" (friendly HM, skeptical Staff engineer). Fix the 3 weakest answers each time.

Day 7Human mock + cheat sheet

Do one mock with a friend or mentor. Then reduce everything to the §18 one-screen cheat sheet you'll read the morning of the interview.

20For mentors: reviewing a mentee's handbook

If you're sharing this guide with people you coach, this is your review checklist.

Run the session like this

  • Minute 0–5: they pitch with the handbook closed. You only listen.
  • 5–25: you drill one hook 3 levels deep, then pick a topic they didn't hook.
  • 25–35: they whiteboard the architecture and sequence diagram from memory.
  • 35–45: one behavioral question from the same project.
  • 45–60: walk through the rubric together; agree on 3 fixes.

Signals you're looking for

  • Ownership is crisp and consistent across answers.
  • They reason through unknowns instead of bluffing.
  • Tradeoff answers name the cost accepted.
  • Numbers survive "how did you measure that?"
  • They stop talking when the answer is done.

Red flags to send back

  • ✕
    AI voice, not their voiceAnswers they can't say naturally, or words they can't define. If they can't explain a term in the handbook, it shouldn't be there.
  • ✕
    Invented or unverified numbersA metric with no "how measured" note. This is the fastest way to lose an interviewer's trust.
  • ✕
    Only Ring 1Deep on their diff, blank on the system around it. Send them to prompt ⑨.
  • ✕
    Only happy pathNo failure modes, no "what if X is down", no scale story.
  • ✕
    Confidential contentReal internal names, customer names, URLs or code in a document they might share.

21FAQ

I only fixed a few bugs. Is that really a "project"?

Yes — if you can explain the system the bug lived in, why it happened, how you found it, why your fix was the right one, and what it changed. A well-understood bug fix beats a poorly-understood "platform rewrite" every time. Use the four rings (§03) to build the context around it.

Is it honest to prepare deeply on parts I didn't build?

Completely — as long as you label them honestly in the interview. Understanding your surrounding system is part of being a good engineer. The dishonest version is claiming you built it. The ownership matrix (§09) exists to keep you on the right side of that line.

Can I put a project on my resume I haven't worked on?

Don't claim work you didn't do. But you can (a) build it yourself as a personal project and present it as such, or (b) present a part you genuinely contributed to, with honest scope. Either way, this playbook works the same: prepare it end-to-end, label ownership truthfully. Interviewers detect fabricated projects quickly, usually at the L3 level.

Which AI model should I use?

The strongest one you have access to, ideally with a large context window, and one your company allows for anything work-derived. Coding agents with repository access (and permission to use them) produce the most accurate Ring 1–2 detail because they can trace the actual code with prompt ⑧.

The AI made up details. What do I do?

That's why rule R1 forces [[TODO]] and "Inferred" labels. Treat the first output as a draft you must verify. Delete anything you can't confirm. A smaller true handbook is worth far more than a bigger fictional one.

How many projects should I prepare like this?

Every project on your resume should survive at least L1–L3. Pick your top 2 for the full 18-section treatment — those are the ones you'll steer interviewers toward.

Should I memorize the answers?

Memorize the structure and the key facts (numbers, names, decisions) — not the sentences. Recited answers sound recited. Rehearse out loud until the content is automatic and the wording is fresh each time.
